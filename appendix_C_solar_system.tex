\section{Science Scope}

The original proposal identifies NEO discovery, physical characterization,
orbit refinement, impact-risk analysis, and early warning as one connected
program. The scientific logic is sound although the hardware requirements
for each stage are significantly different. A defensible mission architecture therefore divides
the program into four products.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=7mm,itemsep=2mm]
\item \textbf{\color{cTeal}Recovery and astrometry.} Measure accurate
positions in the International Celestial Reference Frame (ICRF), the
quasar-defined inertial reference frame used for high-accuracy celestial
astrometry, for recently discovered objects and extend the observational arc
before ephemeris uncertainty grows.
\item \textbf{\color{cGold}Physical characterization.} Measure colors,
spectra, light curves, phase behavior, and thermal flux when a mid-infrared
channel is present.
\item \textbf{\color{cOrange}Dynamical refinement.} Fit gravitational and
non-gravitational accelerations, including Yarkovsky drift when the data arc
and physical priors support the fit.
\item \textbf{\color{cRed}Hazard assessment.} Propagate the full orbit
covariance through close encounters. A nominal trajectory alone is not an
impact probability.
\end{enumerate}

The \facility\ contributes most strongly where aperture, angular resolution,
robotic scheduling, and a stable space environment improve follow-up. A
dedicated discovery survey additionally requires étendue, repeated cadence,
moving-object processing, and access toward the Sun.

\section{Size, Albedo, and Thermal Flux}

For an asteroid with absolute magnitude $H$ and geometric albedo $p_V$, the
diameter proxy is formulated as
\begin{equation}
  D\,[\mathrm{m}]
  =1.329\times10^6\,p_V^{-1/2}10^{-0.2H}.
  \label{eq:h-diameter}
\end{equation}
The equation shows why reflected-light discovery does not determine impact
energy. A dark and large body may have the same $H$ as a bright and smaller
body. Thermal-infrared flux breaks much of this degeneracy and supports a
diameter estimate when the observing geometry and thermal model are known
\cite{Mainzer2011,Mainzer2023}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{appendixC_assets/neo_h_diameter.png}
\caption{Diameter inferred from $H$ depends strongly on the assumed visible
geometric albedo. The plotted relation is Equation~\ref{eq:h-diameter}.}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Why mid-infrared imaging is required}

Mid-infrared imaging is required because reflected light alone does not
provide an independent size measurement. The reflected flux constrains a
combination proportional to $p_VD^2$. Equation~\ref{eq:h-diameter} therefore
leaves the diameter uncertain by $p_V^{-1/2}$. The thermal flux in the
Near-Earth Asteroid Thermal Model has the schematic dependence
\begin{equation}
 F_{\nu}^{\rm th}
 \mathrel{\propto}
 \frac{\epsilon D^2}{\Delta^2}
 B_\nu\!\left(T\right),
 \qquad
 T\mathrel{\propto}
 \left(\frac{1-A}{\eta\epsilon r^2}\right)^{1/4},
 \label{eq:thermal-diameter}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ and $r$ are the observer and heliocentric distances. The
diameter enters the emitted flux through the projected area as $D^2$.
The Bond albedo $A$ enters only through a fourth-root temperature correction
\cite{Harris1998,Mainzer2011}. A measured thermal spectral energy distribution
therefore provides a nearly albedo-independent diameter constraint. The
constraint is independent of the assumed visible geometric albedo $p_V$
although shape, thermal inertia, surface roughness, rotation, and the beaming
parameter $\eta$ remain model uncertainties. Combining the MIR diameter with
$H$ then determines $p_V$ rather than assuming $p_V$. The distinction is
essential for hazard assessment because impact energy scales approximately
as $D^3$.

Wien's displacement law gives
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\rm peak}
  =\frac{b}{T},
  \qquad
  b\simeq2898\,\mu\mathrm{m\,K}.
  \label{eq:wien}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side $\lambda_{\rm peak}$ is the wavelength at which the
blackbody spectral radiance per unit wavelength $B_\lambda(T)$ reaches its
maximum. The symbol $T$ is the effective blackbody temperature in kelvin and
$b$ is Wien's displacement constant. Equation~\ref{eq:wien} returns
$\lambda_{\rm peak}$ in micrometers when $b$ is expressed in
$\mu\mathrm{m\,K}$. The numerical constant applies to the peak of
$B_\lambda$. A spectrum expressed per unit frequency as $B_\nu$ has a
different numerical peak.
Objects at 250--400\,K peak near 11.6--7.2\,$\mu$m. The present
0.2--1.5\,$\mu$m range measures reflected sunlight for most NEOs. It does not
sample the thermal maximum. NEO Surveyor illustrates the required system
design. The mission uses 4.0--5.2 and 6.0--10.0\,$\mu$m channels, a broad
field, a cold optical system, and a field of regard that approaches the Sun
\cite{Mainzer2023}.

\begin{warningbox}
\textbf{\color{cOrange}Cooling is necessary but insufficient.}
A credible discovery upgrade requires a mid-infrared focal plane, cold
fore-optics, low detector dark current, low self-emission, a forward baffle,
a wide field, repeated moving-object cadence, and a data system that links
tracklets. A cold telescope with only the baseline detector remains an
optical and near-infrared observatory.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{Cooled MIR imaging mode and exposure-time calculation}

The proposed discovery option images the same field in two channels. MIR-1
covers 4.0--5.2\,$\mu$m and MIR-2 covers 6.0--10.0\,$\mu$m. The channel
definitions follow NEO Surveyor because the shorter channel constrains the
color temperature while the longer channel covers the thermal emission of
typical 200--300\,K NEOs \cite{Mainzer2023}. The channel choice does not imply
that the NEO Surveyor focal plane or survey design transfers directly to a
3.5\,m segmented telescope.

The MIR imaging ETC integrates the source and background over the full
rectangular passband. A flat $F_\nu$ point source produces
\begin{equation}
 \dot N_{\rm src}
 = A_{\rm eff}\int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}
 F_\nu\frac{c}{\lambda^2}
 \frac{\lambda}{hc}\eta(\lambda)
 {\cal E}(\lambda,r_{\rm ap})\,d\lambda ,
 \label{eq:mir-source-rate}
\end{equation}
Here the left-hand side $\dot N_{\rm src}$ is the detected source
photoelectron rate, in e$^-$\,s$^{-1}$, integrated over the passband and the
photometric aperture. The factor ${\cal E}$ is the wavelength-dependent
encircled-energy fraction inside that aperture. The diffuse-background rate
per detector pixel is
\begin{equation}
 \dot N_{\rm bg,pix}
 = A_{\rm eff}\Omega_{\rm pix}
 \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}
 I_\lambda(\lambda)\frac{\lambda}{hc}
 \eta(\lambda)\,d\lambda .
 \label{eq:mir-background-rate}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side $\dot N_{\rm bg,pix}$ is therefore the detected
background photoelectron rate in one physical pixel, in
e$^-$\,s$^{-1}$\,pixel$^{-1}$; it is not the total background in the source
aperture. The ETC multiplies it by the extraction-aperture pixel count before
forming the noise variance.
The zodiacal input uses the DIRBE convention $\nu I_\nu$. The adopted low and
high levels are 220 and 4200\,nW\,m$^{-2}$\,sr$^{-1}$ in MIR-1 and 1700 and
22,000\,nW\,m$^{-2}$\,sr$^{-1}$ in MIR-2. The values and their variation
across the field of regard are reported by Mainzer et al. \cite{Mainzer2023}
from the Leinert and Wright zodiacal models \cite{Leinert1998}. The nominal
ETC values are the geometric means of each range.

\subsection{NEOMOD3 reference population and thermal flux}

The reference population comes from the public NEOMOD3 Fortran simulator
\cite{Nesvorny2024}. We requested the model number of objects between 30\,m
and 3\,km with seed $-50202002$. The resulting realization contains 344,458
NEOs. The sample contains 19,694 objects larger than 140\,m and 801 objects
larger than 1\,km. These counts agree with the published estimates of
$20,000\pm2,000$ and $830\pm60$ \cite{Nesvorny2024}. The simulation retains
the NEOMOD3 size-dependent two-Rayleigh albedo distribution and the
size-dependent orbital distribution.

The simulator supplies $H$, $a$, $e$, $i$, diameter, and visible albedo.
Longitude of ascending node, argument of perihelion, and mean anomaly were
drawn uniformly with a recorded random seed. The orbits were propagated with
the two-body Kepler solution for the survey-selection calculation. The
authoritative orbit analysis in Section~\ref{sec:calculator-bodies} remains
an N-body calculation. The two-body propagation is adequate for a reference
survey selection function but does not represent close encounters.

Thermal flux uses the Near-Earth Asteroid Thermal Model \cite{Harris1998}.
The adopted emissivity is 0.9 and the reference beaming parameter is
$\eta=1.4$. The subsolar temperature is
\begin{equation}
 T_{\rm SS}=
 \left[
 \frac{(1-A)S_\odot}
 {\eta\epsilon\sigma r^2}
 \right]^{1/4}.
 \label{eq:neatm-temperature}
\end{equation}
The illuminated visible hemisphere is integrated numerically at the actual
phase angle. MIR-1 also includes reflected sunlight with
$p_{\rm IR}=1.6p_V$ clipped to the interval 0.015--0.70. The ratio is a
reference assumption because the validated NEO Surveyor model draws
$p_{\rm IR}/p_V$ and $\eta$ from measured distributions
\cite{Mainzer2023,Masiero2023}.

\section{Baseline Optical and Near-Infrared Program}

\subsection{Astrometry and recovery}

For a well-sampled point-spread function, a useful photon-limit estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\rm cent}
  \simeq \frac{\mathrm{FWHM}}{2.355\,\mathrm{S/N}}.
  \label{eq:centroid}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:centroid} is not the mission astrometric error. Absolute
accuracy also depends on geometric-distortion stability, detector effects,
timing, guide-star errors, chromaticity, source trailing, and the reference
catalog. The mission requirement should therefore specify both centroid
precision and an externally validated ICRF floor.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{appendixC_assets/neo_astrometry.png}
\caption{Photon-limited centroid precision for three representative PSF
widths. The shaded floor is a calibration target, not a demonstrated
performance value.}
\end{figure}

The high-value operational mode is a rapid Target-of-Opportunity sequence
that acquires several short, untrailed exposures, repeats the sequence after
a geometry-dependent interval, and publishes astrometry with a complete
timing and distortion solution. The final cadence must be optimized with
synthetic moving-object injection rather than adopted from a static-source
survey.

\subsection{Composition, rotation, and phase behavior}

The Bus--DeMeo system uses spectra from 0.45 to 2.45\,$\mu$m and defines
24 taxonomic classes \cite{DeMeo2009}. The baseline 0.2--1.5\,$\mu$m
spectrograph therefore provides a useful but incomplete taxonomic interval.
Extension to 2.45\,$\mu$m would improve continuity with the established
classification system. Time-resolved photometry measures rotation, amplitude,
and changing viewing geometry. These quantities constrain shape and spin
priors that enter thermal and Yarkovsky models.

\section{L2 Field of Regard}

At Sun--Earth L2, the Sun, Earth, and Moon occupy nearly the same sky
direction. The geometric solar disk is not the relevant exclusion angle.
Scattered light, sunshade geometry, thermal stability, Earth thermal
emission, lunar light, and allowed spacecraft attitudes set a much larger
keep-out region. The present white-paper visibility calculation adopts a
minimum solar elongation of $60^\circ$.

For a fixed target at ecliptic latitude $\beta$ and solar exclusion angle
$\theta$, the purely geometric annual visibility fraction is
\begin{equation}
 f_{\rm vis}(\beta)=
 \begin{cases}
  1-\dfrac{1}{\pi}
  \cos^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\cos\beta}\right),
  & |\beta|<\theta,\\[2mm]
  1, & |\beta|\ge\theta.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:visibility}
\end{equation}
For $\theta=60^\circ$, Equation~\ref{eq:visibility} gives 243.5 observable
days per year on the ecliptic and full-year access above
$|\beta|=60^\circ$. These values describe geometry only.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{appendixC_assets/l2_observability.png}
\caption{L2 field-of-regard concept. Red marks a $45^\circ$ inner exclusion

NEO discovery benefits from observing at small elongation because Atira and
other interior or Earth-approaching populations spend substantial time close
to the Sun on the sky. A reduction from $60^\circ$ to $45^\circ$ is therefore
scientifically valuable, but it is not a software setting. The change affects
the baffle, sunshade, radiator view, detector background, and allowed roll
angles. Zodiacal emission also rises toward the ecliptic and toward the Sun.
The exposure-time model must use a wavelength-dependent zodiacal map rather
than one scalar sky level.

\subsection{Cadence and five-year reference yield}

The adopted moving-object sequence follows the demonstrated NEO Surveyor
logic \cite{Mainzer2023,Masiero2023}. One visit contains six dithered
integrations with 145\,s net exposure in a 180\,s allocation. Four visits
over approximately eight hours form a tracklet. The same survey region is
sampled again after 13 days to support linking. A valid tracklet requires at
least four detections with $\mathrm{S/N}>5$ and an angular rate between 0.008
and 8 degrees per day \cite{Masiero2023}. The measured tracklet efficiency
of 99 percent and a reference two-tracklet linking efficiency of 95 percent
enter the yield calculation.

The 30$'\times30'$ field covers 0.25\,deg$^2$. A dedicated day retains
21.75 hours after the 2.25-hour downlink allocation reported for NEO
Surveyor \cite{Mainzer2023}. Four visits per field then cover
27.2 unique deg$^2$ per day before the 13-day repeat. The accessible
45--$120^\circ$ longitude range on both sides of the Sun and
$|\beta|\leq40^\circ$ covers 11,051\,deg$^2$. The 13-day fractional coverage
is only 3.20 percent.

The survey emulator propagates every NEOMOD3 object at 13-day intervals for
five years. Each epoch recomputes the L2 geometry, phase angle, optical
magnitude, both NEATM fluxes, position-dependent zodiacal threshold, and
angular rate. Discovery requires two adjacent eligible epochs. Sky tiling
within each 13-day window is represented as a random coverage probability.
The approximation omits focal-plane gaps, confusion, false links, orbit-fit
quality, station keeping, and unplanned downtime.

\section{Orbit Determination and Non-Gravitational Forces}

The orbit state $\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v})$ is propagated with
\begin{equation}
 \ddot{\mathbf r}
 = \sum_i GM_i
 \frac{\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r|^3}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm zonal}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm 1PN}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm SRP}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm PR}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm SW}
 +\mathbf a_{\rm NG}.
 \label{eq:force-model}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Left-hand side.}
The vector $\mathbf r(t)$ is the barycentric position of the target in the
J2000 International Celestial Reference Frame. Its first derivative is the
barycentric velocity $\mathbf v=\dot{\mathbf r}$. The second derivative
$\ddot{\mathbf r}$ is the total acceleration rather than a force. CODES treats
the target as a massless test particle. The target changes neither the
planetary ephemeris nor the motion of another perturber. Time is measured on
the TDB-compatible ephemeris scale. Positions and accelerations are evaluated
in km and km\,s$^{-2}$ within the numerical force routine.

\textbf{Newtonian gravity.}
The index $i$ runs over the Sun, Mercury barycenter, Venus barycenter, Earth,
Moon, Mars barycenter, Jupiter barycenter, Saturn barycenter, Uranus
barycenter, Neptune barycenter, and Pluto barycenter. The default calculation
also includes the 16 massive main-belt perturbers in the JPL SB441-N16
ephemeris. The vector $\mathbf r_i(t)$ is the barycentric ephemeris position
of body $i$. The displacement $\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r$ points from the target
toward the perturber and its cubic norm supplies the inverse-square
acceleration with the correct direction. The coefficient $GM_i\equiv\mu_i$
is the measured gravitational parameter of the perturber. The implementation
reads $\mu_i$ directly from the JPL kernel or its associated constant table.
It does not multiply a separate value of $G$ by a tabulated mass. The direct
use of $\mu_i$ follows the precision structure of modern solar-system
ephemerides.

\textbf{Planetary zonal gravity.}
The term $\mathbf a_{\rm zonal}$ contains the axisymmetric $J_2$, $J_4$, and
$J_6$ corrections for bodies with measured coefficients. CODES evaluates the
Legendre expansion in the time-dependent IAU north-pole direction supplied
by the NAIF planetary constants kernel. The correction is most important
during a close planetary passage. DE440s supplies a planet-system barycenter
for Mars and the outer planets. A precision trajectory within a satellite
system must also load the corresponding planet-center satellite SPK.

\textbf{Relativistic acceleration.}
The term $\mathbf a_{\rm 1PN}$ is the first post-Newtonian correction. CODES
now evaluates the massless-target Einstein--Infeld--Hoffmann (EIH) expression
using every active DE440s/SB441-N16 source. The correction includes source
potentials, velocities, and Newtonian source accelerations; it reduces exactly
to the solar Schwarzschild test-particle expression when the Sun is the only
stationary source \cite{Newhall1983,Tamayo2020}. The source trajectories remain
the prescribed JPL ephemerides rather than being re-integrated by CODES.

\textbf{Solar radiation terms.}
The outward term $\mathbf a_{\rm SRP}$ denotes direct solar-radiation
pressure. Its amplitude is proportional to the solar radiation pressure at
1\,au, the radiation coefficient $C_R$, the area-to-mass ratio, and the
inverse square of heliocentric distance. The term $\mathbf a_{\rm PR}$ is the
velocity-dependent Poynting--Robertson component of the same radiation field.
The Poynting--Robertson acceleration removes orbital energy through radial
and transverse drag. The term $\mathbf a_{\rm SW}$ transfers momentum from a
radial proton wind after subtracting the target velocity from the wind
velocity. The default density is 5\,cm$^{-3}$ at 1\,au and the default radial
speed is 400\,km\,s$^{-1}$. Both quantities and the non-proton momentum factor
are configurable. The model describes a stationary radial wind. It does not
predict transient coronal structures. The three terms vanish in the local
calculation when no credible area-to-mass ratio is supplied.

\textbf{Empirical non-gravitational acceleration.}
The term $\mathbf a_{\rm NG}$ represents accelerations that are constrained
from astrometry but are not predicted reliably from the known shape and
surface state. CODES resolves the term into $A_1$, $A_2$, and $A_3$ along the
heliocentric radial, transverse, and orbit-normal unit vectors. The adopted
amplitude scales as the inverse square of heliocentric distance. The
transverse coefficient $A_2$ provides a compact Yarkovsky sensitivity model
for an asteroid. The radial and normal coefficients also support diagnostic
fits for asymmetric cometary outgassing. The comet option replaces the
inverse-square amplitude by the normalized Marsden water-ice law. A specified
time lag is applied to the osculating heliocentric state with a
universal-variable Kepler shift before the sublimation amplitude is
evaluated. A scientific orbit solution must fit the coefficients, lag, and
covariance to astrometry. Nonzero trial values in CODES measure dynamical
sensitivity and do not constitute a detection of a non-gravitational force.

The solar-only limit, retained behind the
\code{--solar-1pn-only} diagnostic switch, is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf a_{\rm 1PN}
 =\frac{GM_\odot}{c^2r^3}
 \left[
 \left(\frac{4GM_\odot}{r}-v^2\right)\mathbf r
 +4(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf v
 \right].
\end{equation}
The implemented multi-body correction for target $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a_i^{\rm 1PN}={}&
\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\mu_j\mathbf r_{ij}}{r_{ij}^{3}c^2}
\left[
4\sum_{k\ne i}\frac{\mu_k}{r_{ik}}
+\sum_{k\ne j}\frac{\mu_k}{r_{jk}}
-v_i^2-2v_j^2+4\mathbf v_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf v_j \right.\\
&\left.
+\frac{3(\mathbf r_{ij}\!\cdot\!\mathbf v_j)^2}{2r_{ij}^2}
+\frac{\mathbf r_{ij}\!\cdot\!\mathbf a_j^{N}}{2}
\right]\\
&+\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\mu_j}{c^2}
\left[
\frac{\mathbf r_{ij}\!\cdot(4\mathbf v_i-3\mathbf v_j)}
{r_{ij}^{3}}(\mathbf v_i-\mathbf v_j)
+\frac{7\mathbf a_j^{N}}{2r_{ij}}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_j=GM_j$, $\mathbf r_{ij}=\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j$,
$r_{ij}=|\mathbf r_{ij}|$, and $\mathbf v_i$ and $\mathbf v_j$ are
barycentric velocities. The source acceleration
$\mathbf a_j^{N}=\sum_{k\ne j}\mu_k(\mathbf r_k-\mathbf r_j)/r_{jk}^3$
is Newtonian because a post-Newtonian correction inside this factor would
enter only at order $c^{-4}$. The two sums include the Sun, planetary
barycenters, Earth, Moon, and the enabled large asteroids. The Python and
real128 Fortran implementations agree over a two-day Apophis cross-check to
$4.49\times10^{-8}$\,km in position and
$1.12\times10^{-8}$\,mm\,s$^{-1}$ in velocity.

Direct radiation pressure is proportional to area-to-mass ratio. The
Poynting--Robertson term uses the complete first-order photon momentum
equation \cite{Gustafson1996}. The solar-wind term uses the relative velocity
of a configurable radial proton wind. The terms matter most for dust,
meter-scale material, and high area-to-mass artificial objects. For ordinary
NEOs, fitted thermal recoil often dominates the non-gravitational orbit
correction.

\subsection{Yarkovsky and orbit uncertainty}

The transverse parameter $A_2$ is a compact approximation to thermal recoil
when the acceleration scales approximately as $r^{-2}$. The Bennu orbit
solution measured a semimajor-axis drift near $-19\times10^{-4}$\,au\,Myr$^{-1}$
\cite{Chesley2014}. Interpretation requires diameter, density, spin, thermal
inertia, roughness, and shape. YORP-driven spin evolution may also change the
thermal recoil over a sufficiently long interval.

A 100-year nominal integration does not imply a 100-year hazard prediction.
Close encounters map a small initial covariance into a non-Gaussian future
distribution. A hazard result must propagate virtual asteroids or an
equivalent uncertainty representation and report impact probability. JPL
Sentry-II was designed for this nonlinear problem \cite{Roa2021}.

CODES now requests \code{cov=mat} and \code{full-prec=true} from JPL SBDB,
reads the complete covariance at its own solution epoch, and draws a
reproducible correlated ensemble. The sampled dimensions include the six
heliocentric elements and fitted $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$, or time lag when those
parameters occur in the JPL matrix. Every draw is transformed to a
barycentric J2000 state and propagated independently through the same force
model. For JPL Apophis solution 220, the nominal element-to-state conversion
agrees with the independently requested Horizons state at the covariance
epoch to 0.714\,m and $1.43\times10^{-4}$\,mm\,s$^{-1}$. The direct
finite-sample impact fraction is a screening diagnostic, not a replacement
for Sentry-II rare-event searches. Propagating that nominal local state to the
2029 encounter gives 38,042.1\,km versus the CNEOS value 38,011.5\,km, with
local-minus-JPL residuals of $+30.6$\,km and $-228.8$\,s. The covariance path
is therefore operational, but this residual prevents presenting CODES as a
replacement for the registered JPL force model.

\section{CODES Orbit Dynamics and Ephemeris System}

The Close-approach Orbit Dynamics and Ephemeris System
(CODES)\footnote{\url{https://github.com/kjhan0606/CODES.git}} provides the
accompanying high-precision NEO calculator. The local Python package remains
\code{neo\_orbit\_calculator} for command-line compatibility. The integrated
desktop interface groups the calculation into NEO dynamics, comet-orbit
evolution, apparent sky positions, and validation. The NEO dynamics tab keeps
the authoritative JPL product separate from the local sensitivity calculation.

\begin{resultbox}
\textbf{\color{cGold}Authoritative SPK mode.}
The Python interface requests a time-continuous small-body SPK from NASA/JPL
Horizons for the selected interval. A 2026--2126 SPK is the preferred
reproducibility product. SPK segments store piecewise Chebyshev coefficients.
Their polynomial degree is not an orbit-integrator order.
\end{resultbox}

\begin{resultbox}
\textbf{\color{cTeal}Local sensitivity mode.}
A Fortran backend calls CSPICE directly at every force evaluation. The
integrated state, force sums, and adaptive Runge--Kutta arithmetic use
\code{selected\_real\_kind(33,4931)}. GNU Fortran provides 33 decimal digits
on the current platform. SPICE input and output remain IEEE-754 binary64,
which is the precision boundary of the JPL SPK interface.
\end{resultbox}

The phrase ``NASA 100th-order integration coefficients'' does not correspond
to the JPL product. DE440 and DE441 are fitted numerical ephemerides delivered
through SPK Chebyshev segments \cite{Park2021}. Increasing a formal local
integrator order above 100 would not remove initial-state covariance,
close-encounter sensitivity, uncertain thermal recoil, or omitted perturbers.

\subsection{Reproducibility test}

Every release should record the JPL orbit-solution identifier, solution date,
DE and SB kernel names, kernel checksums, TDB epochs, compiler version,
floating-point kind, tolerances, force switches, and source revision. The
local state must be compared with Horizons at an independent epoch grid.
Residuals should be reported before and after close encounters. A residual
threshold must be set by the science case, not by the number of printed
digits.

The real128 Fortran step is selected by both the embedded
Dormand--Prince local-error estimate and a physical close-passage limiter.
For every active source, the internal step is no larger than 5\% of the
gravity timescale $\sqrt{r^3/GM}$ or the relative crossing time
$r/|\Delta\mathbf v|$. High acceleration or high relative speed therefore
forces smaller steps independently of the requested output cadence.

\subsection{Ten historical close-approach tests}
\label{sec:historical-ca-tests}

The regression suite uses ten real NEOs observed between 2019 and 2024.
For each object, the calculator downloads all available optical ADES
astrometry from the Minor Planet Center and retains the observatory codes,
UTC epochs, reported coordinates, and MPC discovery flag
\cite{MPCObservations}. The observing arc is therefore traceable to the
measurements used by the international small-body community.

This test isolates \emph{trajectory propagation}. The current JPL Horizons
solution supplies the barycentric initial state two days before the official
closest-approach epoch. The real128 Fortran model then propagates for four
days with DE440s, SB441-N16, and solar 1PN enabled. A cubic interpolation of
the propagated state locates the Earth-centered distance minimum between
output epochs. The official epoch, miss distance, and relative speed come
from the JPL CNEOS Close-Approach Data API \cite{JPLCAD}. Heliocentric
ecliptic osculating elements are compared with Horizons one day before the
encounter, before the strongest terrestrial deflection.

\subsection{Medium- and long-period comet modes}

CODES also provides two comet products. The \code{comet-orbits} mode
assembles the separate NASA/JPL Horizons solution for each identified
apparition and shows the evolution of the return interval, semimajor axis,
and perihelion distance. The \code{comet-sky} mode accepts specified UTC
epochs and returns the geocentric right ascension, declination, distance, and
IAU constellation. The two products distinguish long-term dynamics from the
position needed to schedule an observation.

A return of 1P/Halley means a passage through perihelion rather than a return
to Earth. The present JPL \#75 solution uses 8,518 observations over
1835--1994. The long-term propagation test takes only its barycentric state
at 1850 January 2 TDB and the published non-gravitational coefficients
$A_1=4.887055233121\times10^{-10}$ and
$A_2=1.554720290005\times10^{-10}$\,au\,d$^{-2}$. CODES then propagates to
2000 with its real128 Fortran integrator, DE440s major-body states,
SB441-N16 perturbers, full multi-body 1PN, and the standard Marsden water-ice law.
No JPL comparison state is ingested after the initial epoch.

compared with the observation-constrained perihelion times of Yeomans and
Kiang \cite{Horizons,YeomansKiang1981}. Those records diagnose period changes
without relabeling an unconstrained modern-state extrapolation as an observed
trajectory.

\section{Recommended Proposal Position}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=7mm,itemsep=2.2mm]
\item Commit the baseline mission to rapid NEO follow-up, ICRF astrometry,
time-resolved photometry, and visible/NIR characterization.
\item Treat the 4--10\,$\mu$m channel as a costed mission option. Specify
detector temperature, cold-optics temperature, field, cadence, elongation,
and survey completeness together.
\item Use four visits over eight hours and a 13-day repeat as the reference
discovery cadence. Reserve additional visits for data loss or targeted
follow-up.
\item Do not claim a competitive blind NEO census with a 0.25\,deg$^2$ field.
The reference calculation yields 659 cataloged $D\geq140$\,m objects over
five years under a full-time MIR allocation. A discovery-led option requires
at least a 1\,deg$^2$ MIR field trade.
\item Preserve a $60^\circ$ baseline solar avoidance requirement until a
thermal and stray-light model demonstrates access to $45^\circ$.
\item Validate orbit products against Horizons SPK output. Use covariance
propagation before making any impact-probability claim.
\item Use $GM$ values and versioned JPL kernels throughout. Record kernel
checksums and orbit-solution identifiers in every derived product.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warningbox}
\textbf{\color{cRed}Claim boundary.}
The present analysis establishes a reproducible reference sensitivity and a
preliminary yield under the requirements in Tables~\ref{tab:mir-etc-requirements}
and \ref{tab:neo-reference-yield}. The result is not a mission completeness
certification. A certified yield requires measured detector performance,
the final field of regard, a deterministic pointing history, focal-plane
gaps, synthetic-source recovery, false-link control, and orbit-quality cuts.
\end{warningbox}
